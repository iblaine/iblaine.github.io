You are standing in front of it

The Funk Board

Rick, zooming. The sign does this between the planes and the ships. Sped up: the wall takes half a minute over it.

It is a wall of 8,448 lights. It watches the planes going over Santa Barbara, the rockets going up from Vandenberg, the cruise ships in the channel, and whatever the businesses downstairs are up to tonight. One card at a time, forever.

Nobody types any of it in. The sign is reading the same public radio traffic that air traffic control and the harbor read, and turning it into something you can watch while you finish a beer.

What goes by

The strips below are not mock-ups. They are real frames, drawn by the same code that drives the wall, scrolling the way the wall scrolls them.

AircraftADS-B

Planes flying over, with the airline, where the flight started, where it lands, the type of aircraft and how many seats it holds. Aircraft broadcast their position in the clear, so if one is above you it is usually on the sign about a minute later. Look up, then look at the wall.

RocketsVandenberg

Vandenberg is up the coast, and it launches often. When one is close the sign stops being polite: at five minutes out it drops whatever it was saying, flashes, and counts down in the middle of the wall. At liftoff it goes all red for two minutes. Then it goes back to talking about planes as though nothing happened.

ShipsAIS + port schedule

Cruise ships calling at Santa Barbara: which ship, whether it is arriving, sitting, or leaving, and how many people it carries. Boats run the same kind of open beacon planes do.

Neighborsa shared sheet

Who is open, who is pouring what, and who has a band on the patio. Every business in the building gets its own card and can change what its card says.

A clockwhen the feed is out

If the connection drops, the sign does not show you an old plane and hope you do not notice. It gives up honestly and shows the time until the data comes back.

How it is built

Two computers, three thousand miles apart, and neither one can do the job alone.

The wall

An ESP32-S3, a microcontroller that costs about as much as a sandwich and has roughly a thousandth of the memory in your phone. It runs four data chains out to 33 panels and spends nearly all of its attention pushing color into 8,448 lights, one frame at a time. It stores nothing and looks nothing up. It asks one question every 30 seconds and draws whatever comes back.

The brain

A small Amazon EC2 instance in a data center in northern Virginia, the cheapest size they rent, half a gigabyte of memory, around three dollars a month. It does all the asking around: which aircraft is that, where did it take off, how many seats, which ship is due in, what does the brewery want to say tonight. Then it hands the wall a finished list.

That split is the whole trick. Doing the lookups on the wall would cost frames, and a sign that stutters is worse than a sign that is slightly behind. It is also why the sign would rather show you a clock than a lie: it keeps no copy of yesterday, so when Virginia goes quiet there is nothing stale for it to fall back on, and it tells you instead.

Questions people actually ask

Why build this?
Just because. Have you seen my Walking Bike?
Did the whole wall just flash red?
Yes. Something left Vandenberg.
Is it watching me?
No. It has no camera, no microphone, and no idea you are standing there. It reads radio from aircraft and ships that are broadcasting on purpose, a launch schedule, and a spreadsheet. The most personal thing it knows is what time it is.
Can you put my flight on it?
Fly over Santa Barbara and it will do that by itself. On a schedule, for a fee, no.
Why is it so slow?
It moves about four pixels a second, which is roughly the reading speed of a person holding a drink. Faster looked frantic. This is a wall, not a notification.
Why does it say Summerland and not Summerland Leather?
The name row on a card holds fourteen characters. The trade did not fit, so the tagline underneath had to carry it. Let's not lose sleep over this.
Did you draw the little pictures?
Yes, one pixel at a time, forty-eight by forty-eight, which is a strange hobby to admit to in writing.
What happens if I unplug it?
It comes back on its own and picks up wherever the world got to while it was out. The part I would rather you did not touch is the power supply, which pushes sixty amps and has opinions.
Can I buy space on it?
No. Ads make babies cry. Ever see a baby cry? It is due to advertising.
